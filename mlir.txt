before MLIR, were there any other attempts at a generalizable multi-level IR framework
mlir.pdf
Attach
Assistant Reply:

Yes. While MLIR crystallized the “multi-level, extensible IR” idea into a coherent, reusable framework, several earlier projects explored parts of the same space—multiple abstraction levels, extensibility, and heterogeneous targets. Notable predecessors and related efforts include:

    Open64 WHIRL
        A classic example of a compiler with multiple fixed IR levels (five levels of WHIRL), from very high-level down to low-level forms. It demonstrated progressive lowering across levels, but the levels were fixed and not designed for out-of-tree extensibility.

    LLVM’s multi-stage backend IRs
        LLVM IR itself is single-level semantically, but the pipeline historically used several internal representations: SelectionDAG, MachineInstr/Machine IR (MIR), MC. This offered staged lowering, but extensibility for high-level domain abstractions was limited, and mixing heterogeneous high-level dialects wasn’t a design goal.

    JVM and .NET IL ecosystems
        Virtual machine bytecodes provide a stable, reusable IR across languages, enabling some cross-language tooling. However, they are essentially single-level VM abstractions, not multi-level or dialect-mixable representations.

    Polyhedral frameworks (e.g., Pluto/Polly/ISL-based flows)
        These “raise” code into a higher-level mathematical model for loop transformations and then lower back. They effectively introduce another level of representation, but the model is specialized (loop nests/static control) and not designed as a general-purpose, composable multi-level IR for arbitrary domains.

    Liquid Metal (Lime) and Delite/LMS
        Liquid Metal (Lime) explored heterogeneous compilation with a co-designed DSL and compiler that mapped high-level managed semantics onto hardware. Delite and Lightweight Modular Staging (LMS) provided a framework for building DSLs that generate efficient code by staging—effectively creating multiple levels of representation during compilation. These emphasized metaprogramming and DSL construction, offering multi-level code generation paths, but were not general IR containers for mixing arbitrary dialects.

    Tapir (LLVM extension for parallelism)
        Extended LLVM IR to model fork-join parallelism. It showed how to embed higher-level semantics into an IR, but remained an extension to a single-level IR rather than a general multi-level framework.

    Domain-specific ML compilers (XLA, TVM, Glow, Halide)
        These systems use multiple internal representations or scheduling languages (e.g., Halide’s algorithm vs. schedule split; TVM’s Relay + TIR). They demonstrate multi-level lowering tailored to tensor algebra and accelerators. However, they are specialized stacks, not general-purpose IR frameworks meant to host arbitrary languages/dialects together.

    Fortran/FIR-like mid-level IRs and language-specific MIR/SIL
        Rust MIR and Swift SIL introduced explicit mid-level IRs for language-specific optimizations before lowering to LLVM. They validated the need for multi-level pipelines but were language-specific rather than general, extensible platforms.

What MLIR contributed that was new

    First-class notion of dialects to organize operations, types, and attributes, and to allow multiple abstraction levels to coexist in the same module.
    Regions and SSA across nested structures enabling structured control, mixed abstractions, and progressive lowering in small steps.
    Declarative infrastructure (ODS, DRR) and optimization interfaces to make transformations reusable across dialects.
    A strong emphasis on composability and interoperability so that polyhedral, tensor, GPU, and language-front-end dialects can be mixed and incrementally lowered.

In short, pieces of the idea existed—fixed multi-level IRs (WHIRL), multi-stage backends (LLVM’s SelectionDAG/MIR), metaprogramming-based multi-level codegen (LMS/Delite), and domain stacks (Halide/TVM/XLA). MLIR’s novelty is making multi-level, mixed-abstraction compilation a first-class, extensible, general framework rather than a collection of specialized or fixed-level pipelines.

why was MLIR split from the llvm project? why didn't llvm just evolve into this
Attach
Assistant Reply:

Short answer: MLIR grew out of LLVM’s community and ideas, but it solves a different class of problems (multiple abstraction levels, mixed dialects, heterogeneous stacks) that couldn’t be added to “LLVM IR as we know it” without breaking core assumptions and workflows. Keeping it as a separate-but-related project let it move fast, experiment, and stay compatible with LLVM as a backend, while avoiding disruption to LLVM’s proven single-IR design.

Key reasons it wasn’t folded “into LLVM IR”

    Different design center
        LLVM IR is a single mid/low-level IR optimized for C/C++-like languages and scalar/vector CPUs/GPUs. It assumes normalized CFG-based control flow, simple types, and whole-module analyses optimized over decades.
        MLIR is an infrastructure for many IRs (“dialects”) at multiple abstraction levels—graphs, tensor algebra, polyhedral loops, AST-like forms, and machine-level dialects—coexisting in the same module, with progressive lowering in small steps.

    Incompatibility with LLVM IR’s core invariants
        Nested regions and mixed levels: MLIR’s first-class regions, blocks-with-arguments (instead of phi nodes), symbolic attributes, and dialect-scoped types/ops don’t fit LLVM IR’s flat CFG and small fixed type/attribute model.
        Open extensibility: MLIR expects out-of-tree operations, types, and attributes to be first-class. LLVM IR purposefully maintains a stable, closed set of instructions and metadata for compatibility and tooling.

    Ecosystem and maintenance concerns
        Stability vs experimentation: LLVM IR is a stable contract for countless compilers, analyzers, and tools. Rapidly evolving it for multi-level, dialect-mixing features would risk breaking that ecosystem.
        Codebase complexity: Turning LLVM into a multi-level framework would significantly increase surface area and maintenance burden for a community focused on its existing strengths.

    Different infrastructure needs
        MLIR emphasizes declarative operation definitions (ODS), declarative rewrites (DRR), dialect interfaces, generic pass management across arbitrary ops, and round-trippable custom syntaxes—capabilities that don’t map cleanly onto LLVM’s existing passes and MIR/SelectionDAG layers.
        Parallel pass execution via “isolated-from-above” regions and symbol-table based globals trades off with LLVM’s whole-module use-def modeling.

    Organizational pragmatics
        Faster iteration: As a separate subproject (under the LLVM umbrella, but not grafted into LLVM IR), MLIR could iterate quickly, accept diverse dialects, and serve domains like ML frameworks, Fortran FIR, polyhedral optimization, and GPU stacks—then lower to the well-supported LLVM backend.
        Clear layering: MLIR can import/export LLVM IR via an LLVM dialect. That keeps a clean boundary: MLIR for multi-level modeling and transformation, LLVM IR for proven codegen, analysis, and backends.

Why LLVM “evolving into MLIR” wasn’t the right path

    It would have required redefining LLVM IR’s purpose from “one solid mid/low-level IR” to “a host for many IRs and levels,” which would dilute its simplicity and stability.
    Many MLIR features (dialects, regions, attributes/types extensibility, declarative definitions/rewrites) would be bolted on awkwardly or break existing assumptions/tools.
    The community relies on LLVM IR’s long-term stability; MLIR’s innovation cadence would be at odds with that.

How they work together

    MLIR commonly lowers to the LLVM dialect, then to canonical LLVM IR, and uses LLVM’s mature optimizers and code generators.
    Projects can mix high-level dialects (e.g., TensorFlow, affine, GPU) and progressively lower within MLIR, then hand off to LLVM where appropriate.

Net effect: Splitting allowed MLIR to rethink compiler infrastructure for multi-level, heterogeneous, and domain-rich compilation without destabilizing LLVM’s successful core. They’re complementary layers rather than competing replacements.

MLIR seems to try to standardize the set of tools people use to build IRs, but how much does it encourage people to use common IRs?
Attach
Assistant Reply:

Short answer: MLIR standardizes the infrastructure you use to build IRs and makes it easy to interoperate, but it does not force a single common IR. Instead, it nudges convergence by making reuse practical and valuable.

How MLIR encourages commonality without mandating it

    Dialects as modular libraries: MLIR’s “dialect” concept lets teams package ops, types, and attributes into reusable modules. Popular dialects (e.g., affine, linalg, tensor, func, scf, arith, gpu, LLVM) become shared building blocks that many projects adopt rather than reinvent.
    Intermixing and progressive lowering: You can mix dialects in the same module and lower gradually. This lowers the cost of adopting shared dialects even if parts of your stack remain custom.
    Declarative definitions and rewrites: ODS (for ops) and DRR (for rewrites) make it cheaper to adopt and extend existing dialects than to fork or rebuild them. Shared canonicalization and folding patterns also encourage consistency.
    Optimization interfaces and generic passes: Many core optimizations (DCE, CSE, canonicalization, inlining, bufferization, vectorization) are written to interfaces and traits, so you benefit more if your ops conform to common traits and dialect conventions.
    Interop dialects: The LLVM, func, scf, arith, and built-in types provide a “lowest common denominator” path that multiple projects converge on for mid/low-level lowering. Similarly, domain formats like ONNX or stable ML IRs can be represented as dialects for round-tripping and gradual alignment.
    Testing, tooling, and docs: Shared dialects come with printers/parsers, verifiers, and documentation generation, making them attractive defaults.

Where MLIR deliberately avoids enforcing a single IR

    No “one true” high-level IR: Different domains need different abstractions (graphs, tensor algebra, polyhedral loops, GPU kernels, language-specific semantics). MLIR avoids prematurely standardizing these and instead makes coexistence cheap.
    Extensibility first: Out-of-tree and experimental dialects are first-class. MLIR treats unknown ops conservatively, so innovation isn’t blocked by central standardization.
    Multiple overlapping “standard” dialects: For control flow and math there are alternatives (e.g., scf vs affine for loops, arith/math vs domain-specific numeric ops). The ecosystem tends to converge where it’s practical, not by fiat.

What this looks like in practice

    Convergence at key layers: Many projects settle on a common mid/low-level path: tensor/linalg → scf/arith/vector → LLVM dialect → LLVM IR. This creates de facto standards for lowerings and codegen reuse.
    Shared domain stacks: In ML/tensor compilers, linalg/tosa/tensor/vector/gpu are common choices. In HPC or polyhedral flows, affine and vector are shared. Language frontends reuse func/scf/CFG patterns and eventually LLVM.
    Room for specialization: Projects still create custom dialects for language semantics, accelerator intrinsics, or novel optimizations, then interoperate with shared dialects where it helps.

Bottom line

    MLIR is pro-standardization of the infrastructure and interfaces, not prescriptive about a single IR. It uses incentives—lower engineering cost, better tooling, and cross-project optimization—to encourage reuse and gradual convergence on common dialects where it makes sense.

are there any non ML libraries that have MLIR as a major part of their compile chain? any languages?
Attach
Assistant Reply:

Yes. Several non–machine-learning projects and languages use MLIR as a major piece of their compilation pipeline:

Languages and general-purpose frontends

    Fortran (Flang/FIR): The LLVM Flang project uses a Fortran-specific MLIR dialect (FIR) for high-level optimizations and then lowers to LLVM for codegen.
    Rust (experimental): There have been community experiments to target MLIR from Rust (e.g., Rust→MLIR prototypes) and to reuse MLIR’s GPU/accelerator paths, though rustc’s production pipeline still targets LLVM IR directly.
    Swift-like and other language IRs: MLIR’s design influenced or has been trialed in various research frontends; some language teams prototype mid-level IRs in MLIR to get canonicalization, bufferization, and GPU lowering “for free,” then lower to LLVM. These are mostly research/experimental rather than production today.

HPC, numerics, and general compilers

    OpenMP and GPU offload: The upstream OpenMP dialect and GPU dialects in MLIR are used in non-ML settings to model and lower heterogeneous kernels (CUDA, ROCm, Vulkan/spirv) from C/C++/Fortran frontends. Projects can compile non-ML numerical kernels through affine/scf/vector/gpu to LLVM or SPIR-V.
    Polyhedral/loop-optimizing toolchains: The affine and vector dialects are used to optimize classic HPC kernels (stencils, dense/sparse linear algebra) independent of ML frameworks.
    Database and query engines (research): Several academic/industrial prototypes compile SQL/relational algebra or dataframe pipelines to MLIR dialects (often custom + affine/vector), then to native code or GPU. These are not mainstream OSS stacks but demonstrate MLIR’s fit beyond ML.

Graphics and shader paths

    SPIR-V and Vulkan: MLIR has a SPIR-V dialect and lowering paths that are used by non-ML shader/transcoding tools and research compilers to generate GPU kernels for graphics/compute via Vulkan.

DSP and embedded

    Domain-specific compilers for signal processing or control often model their IR in MLIR (custom dialects) to gain verification and lowering infrastructure, then target LLVM or hardware-specific backends. Many of these are internal/industrial.

Why most visible adoptions skew toward ML

    Early adopters were ML stacks needing heterogeneous compilation (TensorFlow/XLA, IREE, TOSA/Linalg stacks). That created the impression it’s “for ML,” but the core dialects (func, scf, arith, affine, vector, gpu, LLVM, SPIR-V) are domain-agnostic and are actively used for non-ML kernels.

Summary

    Clear, production-level example: Fortran via Flang/FIR uses MLIR heavily.
    Broad non-ML use: OpenMP/GPU offload, polyhedral/vector optimization, SPIR-V/Vulkan generation, and multiple research/industrial domain compilers (DB, DSP, embedded) build on MLIR even when no machine learning is involved.

does triton interact with mlir at all?
Assistant Reply:

Yes. Modern Triton uses MLIR heavily.

What interacts where

    Frontend: Triton’s Python DSL is parsed to Triton-IR (tt) implemented as an MLIR dialect.
    GPU-specific middle-end: It lowers to TritonGPU (ttg) and vendor-specific dialects (e.g., TritonNvidiaGPU/ttng, TritonAMDGPU) — all MLIR dialects — and applies MLIR passes (CSE, DCE, canonicalization) plus Triton-specific optimizations (coalescing, pipelining, matmul accel, layouts).
    Backend: From those dialects it lowers to the LLVM dialect, then to LLVM IR, and finally to target code (PTX/cubin for NVIDIA, etc.).

Evidence you can check

    Triton switched its backend infrastructure to MLIR in late 2022; current docs list Triton MLIR dialects: tt, ttg, ttng, amdgpu, etc.
    PyTorch’s “Triton Kernel Compilation Stages” blog shows the stages and explicitly notes tt/ttg are MLIR dialects.
    Community talks (Triton Conference 2023/2024; C4ML 2024) and deep-dive posts describe progressive lowering through MLIR dialects to LLVM IR and PTX.
    There are efforts like Triton-Shared (Microsoft) and Triton-CPU that also plug Triton into MLIR-based CPU/other backends.

Bottom line

    Triton is not built on MLIR only at the fringes; its core IRs are MLIR dialects and much of its optimization/lowering is done with MLIR infrastructure before handing off to LLVM for codegen.

are there any full python compilers/runtimes written in mlir?
Assistant Reply:

Short answer: there isn’t a mature, production “full Python” (general CPython-compatible) compiler/runtime entirely built on MLIR that you can drop in today. But there are active and promising projects using MLIR to compile substantial subsets of Python or Python-like languages.

Notable efforts

    Pylir
        What it is: An optimizing ahead-of-time Python compiler built on MLIR, aiming to compile Python to native executables.
        Scope: Ambitious, but still a work-in-progress toward broad Python 3.x compatibility. Implements its own Python-semantics dialects, lowers through MLIR to LLVM.
        Repo: github.com/Pylir/Pylir

    Mojo (Python-like, not CPython)
        What it is: A Python-derived language by Modular, with strong MLIR underpinnings.
        Scope: Not a drop-in Python implementation; rather a new language with Pythonic syntax and systems features. Heavy use of MLIR in the compiler pipeline.

    xDSL (infrastructure)
        What it is: A Python framework for building MLIR-like IRs and passes in pure Python.
        Scope: Not a Python compiler itself, but used in research/teaching and could host Python-compilation experiments.

Related MLIR-based Python-facing stacks (not full Python)

    IREE Turbine + torch-mlir: Compiles PyTorch programs captured from Python to MLIR and on to CPU/GPU backends. This compiles models/functions, not the general Python language.
    Triton: A Python DSL for GPU kernels; its core IRs are MLIR dialects. Again, domain-specific, not general Python.
    NumPy/PyData experiments: Research prototypes compile array-oriented Python subsets to MLIR (often via Numba-like flows), but these are not full-language compilers.

Why a “full Python on MLIR” is hard

    Dynamic semantics: CPython’s highly dynamic object model, reflection, late binding, and C-API compatibility are difficult to optimize without either restricting the language or adding a powerful runtime.
    Ecosystem/API surface: Interop with native C extensions and the CPython C-API is a major barrier for alternative implementations.

Bottom line

    If you need a true general Python replacement today: there isn’t a production MLIR-based one yet.
    If you’re okay with subsets or Python-like languages: Pylir (in-progress) and Mojo (Pythonic language) are the closest examples centered on MLIR.
    For compiling Python-embedded compute (ML, kernels): torch-mlir/IREE and Triton are actively used and mature for their domains.
